Cell Membrane and Transport: Animal vs Plant

Every cell is wrapped in a thin, selective barrier. This lesson shows how the plasma membrane is built and how substances cross it through passive, active, and bulk transport, then compares how animal and plant cells respond to the same watery surroundings.

Why does a wilted lettuce leaf recover when you soak it in water, while a red blood cell placed in pure water bursts?

Plasma Membrane (Fluid Mosaic Model)

Both Cells

The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable barrier only 5 to 10 nanometers thick. The fluid mosaic model, proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972, describes it as a mosaic of phospholipids, cholesterol, proteins, and carbohydrates that can shift sideways, which gives the membrane its fluid character.

Function

Separates the cell interior from its surroundings and controls what enters and leaves. Phospholipid heads are hydrophilic and face the watery fluid on both sides, while the hydrophobic tails face each other in the middle. This oily core blocks ions and large polar molecules, so the cell keeps its internal chemistry stable.

Phospholipids

Form the two-layer bilayer that acts as the basic barrier.

Cholesterol

Tucked between phospholipids in animal cells to help keep the membrane flexible but stable.

Proteins

Integral proteins span the bilayer as channels, carriers, and pumps. Peripheral proteins attach to the surface.

Carbohydrate chains

Attached to lipids and proteins on the outer surface, they act as recognition markers between cells.

Animal vs Plant

Plant cells have a rigid cell wall just outside the plasma membrane. The wall is porous and gives strength, but it is the membrane underneath that decides which substances cross. Plant membranes also contain plant sterols (phytosterols) that play a role similar to cholesterol in animal cells.

Selective Permeability

Both Cells

Because of the hydrophobic core, the bilayer lets some substances slip through easily, slows others, and blocks the rest. This is what "selectively permeable" means: the cell, not chance, decides what gets in.

Crosses easily

Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2), and fat-soluble molecules. Small uncharged molecules like water cross slowly.

Needs a membrane protein

Ions (Na+, K+, Cl-), glucose, amino acids, and other polar molecules cannot dissolve in the oily core.

Needs vesicles

Proteins, large particles, and whole microorganisms are far too big to pass through any protein channel.

Function

Protects the cell by admitting nutrients and keeping harmful materials out, while keeping essential molecules inside. If the membrane lost this selectivity, the cell could not maintain itself.

Simple Diffusion

Passive

Diffusion is the movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration. Small, nonpolar molecules can pass straight through the phospholipid bilayer without any protein and without any energy from the cell. Movement continues until the particles are evenly spread out (equilibrium).

Function

Moves oxygen into cells and carbon dioxide out of them. The rate is faster when the concentration gradient is steeper, the temperature is higher, the molecules are smaller, and the exchange surface is thinner or larger.

Facilitated Diffusion

Passive

Ions and polar molecules cannot cross the oily core, so they use transport proteins. This is still passive transport: the particles move down their gradient and the cell spends no ATP. Two kinds of proteins help:

  • Channel proteins form a water-filled pore that lets specific ions or molecules through. Aquaporins are channels that let water pass at a very high rate.
  • Carrier proteins bind one specific molecule (such as glucose), change shape, and release it on the other side.

Function

Lets essential polar substances enter or leave quickly without energy cost. Because each carrier can only work so fast, the rate levels off once all carriers are busy (saturation).

Active Transport: Moving Uphill

Active

Some substances must be moved uphill, from low concentration to high concentration. This needs energy, which comes from ATP, and a carrier protein acting as a pump. Both animal and plant cells use pumps in the plasma membrane, but they use different ones. You will see each in its own section below.

Function

Builds and maintains concentration differences that cells depend on, such as high potassium inside animal cells or a proton gradient across a plant cell's membrane.

Secondary Active Transport (Cotransport)

Secondary active transport does not use ATP directly. Instead, it uses the energy stored in an ion gradient that a primary pump already built. As the ion flows back down its gradient, it drags another substance along.

Symport

Both substances move in the same direction. Example: sodium and glucose entering intestinal cells together.

Antiport

The substances move in opposite directions. Example: a sodium-calcium exchanger that moves Na+ in while moving Ca2+ out.

It is still active transport because the gradient it depends on was created using ATP. It lets cells pull in valuable molecules such as glucose and amino acids even when they are already more concentrated inside.

Endocytosis

Bulk

Large molecules, particles, and even whole cells cannot squeeze through a protein channel. In endocytosis the membrane folds inward around the material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell. It is a form of active transport, so it needs energy.

  • Phagocytosis ("cell eating"): engulfs solid particles or whole cells, such as a white blood cell taking in bacteria.
  • Pinocytosis ("cell drinking"): takes in droplets of fluid and the small molecules dissolved in it.
  • Receptor-mediated endocytosis: specific molecules bind to receptor proteins first, so the cell takes in large amounts of exactly what it needs.

Function

Brings large materials into the cell for digestion or use. The vesicle usually fuses with a lysosome in animal cells, where its contents are broken down.

Animal vs Plant

Plant cells can also take material in by endocytosis, but the rigid cell wall generally prevents them from engulfing large particles the way an animal white blood cell does.

Exocytosis

Bulk

Exocytosis is the reverse of endocytosis. A vesicle, often made by the Golgi apparatus, travels to the plasma membrane, fuses with it, and releases its contents outside the cell. It also adds the vesicle's membrane to the plasma membrane. Like endocytosis, it requires energy.

Function

Exports materials the cell has made, such as digestive enzymes, hormones, and neurotransmitters released between nerve cells, and also removes waste.

Animal vs Plant

In plant cells, Golgi vesicles carry building materials to the plasma membrane by exocytosis so they can be added to the growing cell wall. This links transport back to the Golgi apparatus you studied in Module 01.

The Big Comparison: Osmosis

Osmosis and Tonicity

Passive

Osmosis is the diffusion of water through a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves from the side with less dissolved solute (more free water) to the side with more solute. Tonicity describes how the solute concentration of the surrounding fluid, compared with the inside of the cell, changes the cell's volume.

Function

Osmosis balances water between a cell and its surroundings. Depending on the surrounding solution, water may enter the cell, leave the cell, or move in both directions at equal rates with no net movement.

Animal Cell

Animal cells have a plasma membrane but no rigid cell wall. In a hypotonic solution, water enters and the cell swells, possibly bursting (lysis). In an isotonic solution, it keeps its usual volume. In a hypertonic solution, water leaves and the cell shrivels (crenation).

Plant Cell

Plant cells have a plasma membrane, a rigid cell wall, and a large central vacuole. In a hypotonic solution, water enters and the cell becomes turgid. In an isotonic solution, it becomes flaccid. In a hypertonic solution, the protoplast shrinks and pulls away from the cell wall, a process called plasmolysis.

Why do they respond differently?

The plant cell wall resists the pressure created as water enters, helping prevent the cell from bursting and making the cell firm. Animal cells do not have this rigid wall, so their volume changes more directly with water movement.

Animal Cell Emphasis

Sodium-Potassium Pump

Animal Emphasis

The sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+-ATPase) is a primary active transporter found in the plasma membrane of almost every animal cell. It uses ATP directly to move ions against their gradients.

Function

Each cycle moves 3 sodium ions out of the cell and 2 potassium ions into the cell, using one ATP. This keeps sodium low and potassium high inside the cell and creates an electrical difference across the membrane that is important for nerve impulses.

Why it matters for water balance

An animal cell has no wall to hold its volume in check, so it must control its own solute content. By constantly pumping sodium out, the pump helps keep the inside of the cell from accumulating solutes that would draw water in and make it swell.

Plant Cell Emphasis

Cell Wall and Turgor Pressure

Plant Emphasis

The cell wall is a rigid layer of cellulose fibers outside the plasma membrane. It is porous, so water and small solutes pass through it freely. That means the membrane underneath is still the real selective barrier, while the wall sets a limit on how far the cell can expand.

Function

When water enters a plant cell, the contents push against the wall. This turgor pressure rises until it balances the water's tendency to enter, so the cell stops swelling instead of bursting. Turgor holds leaves and non-woody stems upright, and losing it is what we see as wilting.

Turgid

Firm and healthy. Water has entered and the cell contents press against the wall.

Flaccid

Limp. There is little pressure on the wall, so tissues begin to wilt.

Plasmolyzed

The membrane has pulled away from the wall. It can recover early on, but prolonged plasmolysis can kill the cell.

Central Vacuole and Tonoplast

Plant Emphasis

A mature plant cell usually has one large central vacuole that can take up to 90% of the cell's volume. Its surrounding membrane, the tonoplast, contains water channels (aquaporins) and pumps that move water and ions between the vacuole and the cytoplasm.

Function

Acts as the cell's water reservoir. Most of the water that enters a plant cell goes into the vacuole, which builds turgor pressure, and it is also the first compartment to shrink when the cell loses water. The vacuole also stores ions, sugars, and wastes.

Animal vs Plant

Animal cells have only small vacuoles and vesicles, so they have no large water store to buffer them. Their volume depends much more directly on solute balance across the plasma membrane.

Proton Pump (H+-ATPase)

Plant Emphasis

Where animal cells lean on the sodium-potassium pump, plant cells depend heavily on a proton pump in the plasma membrane. It uses ATP to push hydrogen ions (H+) out of the cell.

Function

Builds a proton gradient and an electrical difference across the membrane. Secondary transporters then use that stored energy to bring nutrients such as sugars and nitrate into the cell, the same cotransport idea you saw earlier.

Why the difference?

Both pumps do the same job: they use ATP to build a gradient that powers other transport. Animal cells build it with sodium and potassium, while plant cells build it with protons.

Key Takeaways

  • The plasma membrane is a fluid mosaic of phospholipids, cholesterol, proteins, and carbohydrates that is selectively permeable.
  • Passive transport (diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis) moves substances down their gradient with no ATP.
  • Active transport uses ATP and pumps to move substances against their gradient. Animal cells rely on the sodium-potassium pump; plant cells rely on the proton pump.
  • Bulk transport (endocytosis and exocytosis) moves large materials in vesicles and needs energy.
  • In pure water an animal cell can burst, but a plant cell becomes turgid because its cell wall pushes back.
  • In a salty solution an animal cell shrivels (crenation), while a plant cell's membrane pulls away from its wall (plasmolysis).
