Mitosis: Cell Division in Animal & Plant Cells

Every living organism grows and repairs itself through cell division. This lesson examines mitosis — the process by which one cell produces two genetically identical daughter cells — and compares how this process differs between animal and plant cells through 3D visualization.

The Cell Cycle

Interphase

Both Cells

Interphase is the longest phase of the cell cycle — the period in which the cell actively grows, carries out its normal metabolic functions, and prepares for division by duplicating its DNA and organelles. It is divided into three sub-phases: G1 (cell growth and protein synthesis), S (DNA replication, producing identical sister chromatids), and G2 (continued growth and final preparation for division). By the end of interphase, the cell has duplicated its entire genome but has not yet begun to divide its nucleus.

Mitosis Stages

Prophase

Both Cells

Prophase is the first and longest stage of mitosis. Loose chromatin condenses into visible, distinct chromosomes — each consisting of two identical sister chromatids joined at their centromere. The nucleolus disappears and the mitotic spindle begins to form. In animal cells, centrosomes (containing centrioles) migrate to opposite poles and radiate aster fibers. In plant cells, spindle formation is directed by microtubule-organizing centers (MTOCs) without centrioles, and a unique preprophase band first marks the future division site.

Metaphase

Both Cells

Metaphase is the stage in which all chromosomes are fully condensed and precisely aligned along the cell's equator — the metaphase plate. The nuclear envelope has fully broken down, and spindle fibers from opposite poles attach to kinetochore proteins on each chromosome's centromere. A spindle assembly checkpoint verifies that every kinetochore is properly attached before the cell proceeds, ensuring each daughter cell will receive exactly one copy of every chromosome.

Anaphase

Both Cells

Anaphase is the stage of separation. The protein links (cohesins) holding sister chromatids together are cleaved by the enzyme separase, making each chromatid an individual chromosome. Kinetochore microtubules shorten to pull chromosomes toward opposite poles while non-kinetochore microtubules elongate, pushing the poles further apart and visibly stretching the cell. By the end of anaphase, each pole holds a complete, identical set of chromosomes — any segregation error here results in daughter cells with the wrong chromosome number (aneuploidy).

Telophase

Both Cells

Telophase is the reversal of prophase. Chromosomes arrive at opposite poles and begin to decondense back into loose chromatin. A new nuclear envelope reassembles around each chromosome set, the nucleolus reappears in each forming nucleus, and the spindle apparatus disassembles. By the end of telophase, nuclear division (karyokinesis) is complete — the cell contains two genetically identical nuclei but remains a single cell until cytokinesis physically separates the cytoplasm.

Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis

Both Cells

Cytokinesis is the physical division of the cytoplasm that produces two separate daughter cells, typically overlapping with telophase. While both cell types produce two genetically identical daughters, the mechanism differs fundamentally. In animal cells, a contractile ring of actin and myosin filaments pinches the membrane inward — forming a cleavage furrow — until the cell is split in two. In plant cells, the rigid cell wall prevents this, so Golgi-derived vesicles are guided to the cell's center by the phragmoplast, fusing to form a cell plate that grows outward until it meets the existing cell wall.
