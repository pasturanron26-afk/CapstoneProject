Cell Signaling and Communication: Animal vs Plant

Cells do not work alone. They send chemical messages, receive them, and pass small molecules directly to their neighbors. This lesson shows how a signal is received and relayed inside a cell, then compares how animal cells connect through gap junctions and plant cells connect through plasmodesmata.

A hormone flows past thousands of cells, yet only some respond. How does a cell know the message is for it, and how do neighboring cells share messages directly?

Shared Foundation (Both Cells)

Forms of Cell Signaling

Both Cells

Cell signaling means one cell releasing or displaying a signal that another cell detects and responds to. Signals are grouped by how far they travel and who receives them. The cell that responds is the target cell. A cell responds only if it has the right receptor, a protein that fits the signal like a lock fits a key.

Function

Signaling coordinates the body or plant: it times growth and division, controls metabolism, triggers defense, and keeps cells working together instead of each acting alone.

Animal vs Plant

Plants use the same ideas. Plant hormones can act on nearby cells or travel long distances in the phloem. For example, the flowering signal FT protein is made in leaves and moves through the phloem to the shoot tip.

Receptors and Signal Transduction

Both Cells

Signaling has three stages. In reception, the signal binds a receptor. In transduction, the message is passed along a chain of molecules inside the cell, often with second messengers such as cAMP and with enzymes that add phosphate groups (kinases). In the response, the cell changes what it does, for example by switching genes on or activating enzymes.

Function

Transduction converts a signal outside the cell into a response inside it, and it amplifies the message: one signal molecule can lead to thousands of activated enzymes.

Receptor on the surface

Water-soluble signals (such as adrenaline or insulin) cannot cross the lipid bilayer, so they bind receptors in the plasma membrane.

Receptor inside the cell

Small or fat-soluble signals (such as steroid hormones, or auxin entering the nucleus) can enter the cell and bind receptors inside.

Animal Cell Emphasis

Gap Junctions

Animal Emphasis

A gap junction is a cluster of channels that directly connects the cytoplasm of two neighboring animal cells. Each channel is made of two connexons, one from each cell, and each connexon is built from six connexin proteins. The channel is narrow, so ions and small molecules (up to about 1,000 daltons) can pass, but proteins cannot.

Function

Gap junctions let cells share ions, second messengers (such as cAMP), and small metabolites, so groups of cells can act together. In heart muscle, they let electrical signals spread quickly so the heart beats in time. Raised calcium or lower pH closes the channels, which helps cut off a damaged cell from its neighbors.

Plant Cell Emphasis

Plasmodesmata

Plant Emphasis

A cell wall separates neighboring plant cells, so plants use plasmodesmata: narrow channels that pass through the wall and are lined by the plasma membrane. A strand of endoplasmic reticulum, the desmotubule, runs through the middle. Together, the connected cytoplasm of a plant's cells forms one network called the symplast.

Function

Plasmodesmata move water, ions, sugars, and hormones between cells, and the channel can be regulated. During development, it can widen to let some RNAs and proteins through. Depositing callose at the opening narrows or plugs it, which can isolate a damaged or infected cell. Some plant viruses use special movement proteins to open plasmodesmata and spread.

Why different channels?

Animal cells touch each other's membranes directly, so a short protein channel can span the gap between them. Plant cells are separated by a thick wall, so a channel has to run through the wall itself, and the plant lines it with the cell's own membrane and ER.

Plant Hormones and Receptors

Plant Emphasis

Plants do not have a bloodstream or nerves, so they coordinate growth and defense with hormones. Plant hormones act in tiny amounts, and each one affects many processes depending on the tissue and on the other hormones present. Plant cells detect them with receptors in the plasma membrane (many are receptor kinases), in the endoplasmic reticulum membrane (the ethylene receptors), or in the nucleus (the auxin receptor).

Function

Plant hormones coordinate growth, development, and responses to light, water, and attack, so the whole plant acts as one organism even though its cells cannot move.

Auxin

Promotes cell elongation and bends shoots toward light. A high auxin-to-cytokinin ratio favors roots in tissue culture.

Cytokinin

Promotes cell division. A high cytokinin-to-auxin ratio favors shoots in tissue culture.

Gibberellin

Promotes stem elongation and seed germination.

Ethylene

A gas that promotes fruit ripening and leaf drop.

Abscisic acid

Closes stomata during drought and keeps seeds dormant.

Key Takeaways

  • A cell responds to a signal only if it has the matching receptor.
  • Signaling has three stages: reception, transduction, and response. Transduction often amplifies the signal.
  • Signals are grouped by distance: endocrine (far), paracrine (near), autocrine (self), juxtacrine (contact), and direct (through channels).
  • Animal cells connect directly through gap junctions, which pass ions and small molecules but not proteins.
  • Plant cells connect through plasmodesmata that cross the cell wall. Their opening can be widened or plugged with callose.
  • Plants use hormones such as auxin, cytokinin, gibberellin, ethylene, and abscisic acid to coordinate the whole plant.
