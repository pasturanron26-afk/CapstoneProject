Cell Respiration

Cells cannot use the energy in glucose directly. Cellular respiration breaks glucose down in stages, capturing its energy in ATP. This lesson walks through all four stages of aerobic respiration, tallies the ATP produced, and covers what happens when oxygen runs out.

Overall equation

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP (energy)

A Common Misconception

Both animal and plant cells respire. Every living plant cell has mitochondria and breaks down glucose for energy around the clock, whether or not light is available. Photosynthesis only adds a second process, in the chloroplasts, that plant cells run during the day.

Stage 1 & 2: Glycolysis and Pyruvate Oxidation

Glycolysis

Both Cells

Glycolysis ("sugar splitting") is the first stage of respiration and the only stage that happens outside the mitochondrion, in the cytoplasm. It runs whether or not oxygen is present, which is why it is the one stage every living cell, and every anaerobic organism, can rely on.

Function

Splits one 6-carbon glucose molecule into two 3-carbon pyruvate molecules over a 10-step pathway. The first steps invest 2 ATP to destabilize glucose; the later steps generate 4 ATP and 2 NADH, for a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose.

Pyruvate Oxidation (Link Reaction)

Both Cells

Before the pyruvate made in glycolysis can enter the Krebs cycle, it is transported into the mitochondrial matrix and converted into acetyl-CoA. This short step links glycolysis to the Krebs cycle, which is why it is often called the link reaction.

Function

Each pyruvate loses one carbon as carbon dioxide and combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl-CoA, reducing one NAD+ to NADH in the process. Since each glucose produced two pyruvate molecules, this step happens twice per glucose.

Stage 3: The Krebs Cycle

Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle)

Both Cells

The Krebs cycle finishes breaking down the carbon skeleton that started as glucose. Acetyl-CoA combines with a 4-carbon molecule (oxaloacetate) to form 6-carbon citrate, then a series of eight reactions strips away carbon atoms as CO2 and harvests high-energy electrons, regenerating oxaloacetate so the cycle can run again.

Function

Each turn of the cycle releases 2 CO2 and produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 ATP (made as GTP). Because each glucose yields 2 acetyl-CoA molecules, the cycle turns twice per glucose.

Stage 4: Electron Transport Chain and Chemiosmosis

Electron Transport Chain & Chemiosmosis

Both Cells

The electron transport chain (ETC) is a series of protein complexes (I to IV) built into the inner mitochondrial membrane. NADH and FADH2 deliver the electrons they have been carrying since glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. As electrons pass from complex to complex, energy is released and used to pump hydrogen ions (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space, building a steep concentration gradient.

Function

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor: at Complex IV, oxygen combines with electrons and H+ to form water. This is why oxygen is required for this stage, and why cells suffocate without it.

The buildup of H+ in the intermembrane space is the proton-motive force. Hydrogen ions flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, and that flow turns the enzyme like a tiny turbine, joining ADP and inorganic phosphate into ATP. This coupling of the electron transport chain to ATP production is called oxidative phosphorylation, and it produces roughly 90 percent of the ATP made during aerobic respiration.

When Oxygen Runs Out

Anaerobic Respiration (Fermentation)

Anaerobic

Without oxygen, the electron transport chain backs up: NADH cannot unload its electrons, so NAD+ is not regenerated, and glycolysis grinds to a halt. Fermentation is a short pathway that regenerates NAD+ from NADH so glycolysis can keep making its small but immediate supply of ATP.

Function

Fermentation itself makes no additional ATP. Its only job is to regenerate NAD+, so the net yield without oxygen is just the 2 ATP from glycolysis, far less than the ~30 to 32 ATP available with oxygen.

Animal vs Plant

Muscle cells produce lactic acid during intense exercise, when oxygen delivery cannot keep up. Yeast and many plant cells produce ethanol and carbon dioxide instead, most notably in plant roots left waterlogged and short of oxygen, and in yeast during baking and brewing.

Key Takeaways

  • Aerobic respiration has four stages: glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain with chemiosmosis.
  • Only glycolysis happens outside the mitochondrion; the rest take place in or on the inner mitochondrial membrane.
  • Most ATP, roughly 26 to 28 molecules per glucose, comes from oxidative phosphorylation, not from the earlier stages directly.
  • Oxygen's only job is to accept electrons at the end of the chain; without it, the chain stops and cells switch to fermentation.
  • Fermentation regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue, but it yields far less ATP than aerobic respiration.
  • Both animal and plant cells carry out cellular respiration in their mitochondria.
