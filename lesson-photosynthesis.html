Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is how plant cells capture light energy and store it as chemical energy in glucose. This lesson covers where photosynthesis happens, how the light-dependent reactions and the Calvin cycle work together, and what limits how fast a plant can photosynthesize.

Overall equation

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Plant Cells Only

Photosynthesis happens only in cells with chloroplasts, mainly plant cells and algae. Animal cells have no chloroplasts and cannot photosynthesize; every organelle in this lesson exists only on the plant side of the Animal Cell vs Plant Cell comparison in Module 01.

Where Photosynthesis Happens

Leaf and Chloroplast Structure

Plant Only

In most plants, photosynthesis happens mainly in the mesophyll cells of the leaf, which are packed with chloroplasts. Each chloroplast is bound by a double membrane and contains a fluid-filled interior called the stroma, where the Calvin cycle takes place.

Function

Suspended in the stroma are stacks of flattened, membrane-bound sacs called thylakoids; a stack is called a granum. The thylakoid membrane holds the pigments and protein complexes that capture light energy, so the light-dependent reactions happen there, physically separate from the Calvin cycle in the stroma around them.

The Big Picture

The Light-Dependent Reactions

Light-Dependent Reactions

Plant Only

Chlorophyll and other pigments in Photosystem II absorb light and use the energy to split water molecules, a process called photolysis. This releases oxygen as a byproduct, the same oxygen we breathe, and produces high-energy electrons that enter an electron transport chain.

Function

As electrons pass along the chain toward Photosystem I, their energy pumps hydrogen ions into the thylakoid lumen, building a gradient. Those ions flow back out through ATP synthase, generating ATP by chemiosmosis, just as in cellular respiration. Photosystem I re-energizes the electrons with a second photon of light and passes them to NADP+, forming NADPH.

The Calvin Cycle

Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)

Plant Only

The Calvin cycle does not use light directly, but it depends entirely on the ATP and NADPH the light reactions just produced. It runs in the stroma and builds carbohydrate one carbon at a time, in three stages.

  • Fixation: the enzyme RuBisCO attaches a CO2 molecule to RuBP (a 5-carbon sugar), forming two molecules of 3-PGA.
  • Reduction: ATP and NADPH convert 3-PGA into G3P, a 3-carbon sugar.
  • Regeneration: most of the G3P is recycled, using more ATP, to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can continue.

Function

The cycle must turn three times, fixing three CO2 molecules, to produce one net G3P that leaves the cycle. It takes six turns, and six CO2 molecules, to build enough G3P for one glucose molecule.

What Limits the Rate of Photosynthesis

Limiting Factors

Plant Only

The rate of photosynthesis rises as light intensity or carbon dioxide concentration increases, but only up to a point. Once another factor becomes scarce, the rate levels off no matter how much more light or CO2 is supplied. Whichever factor is in shortest supply at a given moment is called the limiting factor.

Function

Temperature also affects the rate, because the reactions of the Calvin cycle depend on enzymes such as RuBisCO. Very high temperatures can denature these enzymes and reduce the rate even if light and CO2 are plentiful.

Practical Note

This is why greenhouses often add extra CO2 and control temperature: once light is no longer the limiting factor, boosting CO2 lets photosynthesis run faster than it would outdoors.

Connecting to Respiration

Photosynthesis and Respiration Together

Both Processes

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are, in a sense, opposite processes that a plant cell runs at the same time. Photosynthesis builds glucose and releases oxygen using light, carbon dioxide, and water. Respiration then breaks that same glucose back down, using the oxygen released by photosynthesis, to release ATP, carbon dioxide, and water.

Function

A plant cell contains both chloroplasts and mitochondria and needs both processes. It photosynthesizes only while light is available, but it respires around the clock to keep its cells powered, even at night.

Key Takeaways

  • Photosynthesis happens only in chloroplasts, found in plant cells and algae, never in animal cells.
  • The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane, split water, release O2, and produce ATP and NADPH.
  • The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma, uses that ATP and NADPH, and fixes CO2 into G3P to eventually build glucose.
  • Light intensity, CO2 concentration, and temperature can each act as the limiting factor on the rate of photosynthesis.
  • Plant cells both photosynthesize (only in light) and respire (constantly), using chloroplasts and mitochondria together.
